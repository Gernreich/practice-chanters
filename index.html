A Great Highland bagpipe practice chanter in 3 mm plywood with an octagonal bore: the stacked octagon, a stack of thin cross-section plates with the bore cut through every one, clamped on two threaded rods, under a stacked cap and mouthpiece; its holes are a real practice chanter's, where that chanter's are. Three more are built differently, from long strips glued round the bore: the strip chanter, whose holes come from the model, the standard strip chanter, whose holes are a real practice chanter's, and the standard octagon, the same holes as slots in eight strips round an octagonal bore. Besides threaded rod, nuts and pins, the only part you buy is a plastic practice-chanter reed.

Output is millimetre-true — 1 user unit = 1mm with a physical width/height — so it prints and cuts at real size.

None has been cut or played yet. Three of the four copy a measured chanter's holes and use an acoustic model, not yet checked against a real chanter, for the difference; the strip chanter's holes come from the model alone. The reed's length inside the cap is a guess. Before you cut says what that costs, and Tuning how to take it up.

Why an octagon

Stacked plates take any polygon. What a bore's cross-section needs is the right area. Its shape matters far less: a square-bored chanter was built on exactly that principle, a square of the round bore's area at every point. Here the bore is the area of the measured practice chanter's 4.87 mm round bore, 18.63mm². A plate is a slice across the chanter, so the polygon is simply cut out of it.

Until 2026-10-23 there were six stacked chanters, triangle to octagon, all of which built and tuned in the model. Only the octagon is kept:

  • Its bore is all but round. Each face is 1.96 mm wide, where a triangle's of the same area would be 6.56 mm and a square's 4.32 mm, and the laser's own kerf rounds its corners.
  • It has the least wall. For the same area it has 3% more wall than a round bore, against 13% for the square and 29% for the triangle. More wall means more friction losses, and so duller, less responsive notes.

The model still takes any number of sides from three to eight: target/release/chanter_model --sides=5 solves the pentagon's holes.

There is a cut-off if the bore is built the other way, from long strips jointed round it as the octagonal flute is. Each strip is a face of the bore, and its finger joints need ply between them. In 3 mm ply at a 4 mm bore, only the square works. The pentagon leaves 1.36 mm between joints where 3 mm is needed. The hexagon and up leave less, and the octagon none. The triangle cannot be finger-jointed at all, since its corners are too sharp for the joint. Thinner 1.5 mm stock buys the pentagon. A wider bore would buy more shapes, but then it is no longer a practice chanter. That is why this one is a stack.

The stacked octagon

Its holes are the measured practice chanter's, as the two standard chanters' are: each where the reference's is, measured from the reed's seat, and sized in the model to sound the reference's note there. Until 2026-10-23 the model placed and sized them on its own, round a 4 mm bore inside a 20 mm outside, and gave a body 396 mm long, half as long again as the real chanter's. Its bore was a third smaller than the reference's and its wall thicker.

A hole is a slot across one plate, 3 mm tall and as wide as its note needs, from the bore out through the face. The model takes it as the round hole of its area. Each sits in the plate the reference's hole centre falls in, so it is up to 1.5 mm from the reference's, 1.0 mm at the most here. A slot under 1 mm wide would be too narrow to cut cleanly and would be drilled instead, at a V cut in its plate's edge; at 3 mm ply none is.

The outside is as thin as the rods allow. Each plate carries two M2 rods, which need 1.5 mm of ply round them, clear of the bore, the seat and any slot, and room for a washer on the end plates. At 15.5 mm across the flats they would have less. A thicker wall wants bigger holes: at 17 mm low A would need a slot wider than 5 mm, more than a finger pad closes. 16 mm is between the two, and the reference's own outside, about 20 mm at low A and 15 mm at high A, spans it.

The staple is held by its hemp, as on the reference. The bore under the seat is wider than the smallest staple, so nothing stops one but the hemp wrapped round it.

Low G sounds from the whole bore, and the bore is a whole number of plates, so it lands 6.7 cents sharp: a plate less would be 21 cents sharp, a plate more 8 flat. The generator prints how far.

The strip chanter

The second design builds the square bore from four long strips of 3 mm ply, front, back and two sides, glued round it. The strips run from the sole to just above the high A hole. Above that the chanter is stacked plates, the cookies, up to the reed's seat.

Bore 3.54 mm square in the strips, 4 mm round in the cookies: the same area, so no note moves
Strips 259.5 mm long, 9.54 mm square outside; finger-jointed (--joint=finger, the default) or butt-jointed (--joint=butt)
Cookies 54 plates, 20 mm square: 5 glued over the strips' top, 43 with the round bore, 6 for the seat; clamped on two M2 rods 169 mm long
Where it changes 244 mm up the 406.5 mm body, 0.60 of the way; high A's centre 10.0 mm under the cookies
Holes round, laser-cut in the strips, 1.02 to 3.09 mm across; the thumb's in the back strip
Model every note within 1.0 cents, low G too, since the strips can be any length
Sheet one 599 × 157 mm sheet, 101 parts

Finger joints do work. The strips are 3 mm thick round a 3.54 mm bore. At each corner one strip has a finger 3 mm deep and its neighbour a notch. Each strip has fingers along one edge or the other in turn, so it is seldom only its 3.54 mm middle. Round each hole its own strip has fingers along both edges, 1.5 mm past the hole, so the ply beside a hole is never a notch. The strip across from it then has none, for 4 to 10 mm: the back strip opposite each front hole, and the front opposite the thumb's. Glued into the square, those stretches are held on all four sides. The fingers also line the strips up as they go together, which butt joints leave to you. With --joint=butt the front and back are 9.54 mm wide and the sides 3.54 mm, glued between them.

Why the holes are small. The holes are spaced for the hands, 27 mm apart in each hand, but the scale's steps are not equal, so each hole is whatever size puts its note in tune where it stands. A hole for a small step, such as D or G, comes out small. The largest is limited by the bore: a hole wider than the bore's 3.54 mm face would cut into the side strips, so 3.2 mm is the most. The hand's layout is set by C and E, which are solved at 2.5 mm and pull every other hole along with them. The wall's thickness matters little: with an 8 mm wall the holes are within 0.4 mm of these. It does matter for G. F to high G is the scale's smallest step, so G must sit 16 mm above F instead of 21 mm. Any further and high G would be sharp even at a 1 mm hole.

The thumb has room. The cookies stand 5 mm proud of the strips. The high A hole's centre is 10.0 mm below that shoulder, so the thumb can seal it. Pass --switch=0.8 to put the change 4/5 of the way up instead.

The staple stops on its hemp. The round bore under the seat is 4 mm, the size of the smallest staple, so a 4 mm staple could slide on into it. A larger one stops on the bore.

What is glued. The strips to each other, and the strips into the cookies' socket. The cookies are clamped on rods, and the cap press-fits as on the stacked octagon.

Building it:

  1. Cut the sheet. Dry-fit the four strips first: each strip's two edges have different finger patterns, so it goes in only one way.
  2. Glue the strips round a length of 3.5 mm rod or a waxed drill bit, to keep glue out of the bore. Clamp lightly and wipe off any glue that squeezes out.
  3. Thread the cookies onto the two rods, from the seat down to the socket, with a nut and washer at the seat end. Brush shellac on each face as it goes on.
  4. Glue the strips' top 15 mm into the socket. Put the bottom washers and nuts on and tighten them. Cut the rods flush with the top nuts.
  5. Seal the bore with shellac on a pipe cleaner, and fit the cap and reed as on the stacked octagon.

The standard strip chanter

This one takes its holes from a real practice chanter: aero1016's model of the practice chanter they own, measured from its 3D file. That chanter has a 4.87 mm round bore, 238.5 mm from the sole to the reed's seat, its front holes 25 mm apart from B up to F and low A 32 mm below B, and a collar 10 mm under the seat that the top rests on.

Bore 5.5 mm square, from the sole into the collar
Strips 236.3 mm long, 11.5 mm square outside, finger-jointed (--joint=butt for butt joints), glued
Foot two layers of four strips lying flat round the strips' bottom, each cut to a 50 mm circle: 19.25 mm wide mid-face, shorter to the corners; 6 mm tall. The reference's foot is 25.4 mm round and 19 mm tall, so this one is wider and lower
Band three 10 mm strips glued round the strips' front and sides, under the collar; none on the back, and each side piece's lower back corner cut away along a 10 mm quarter circle up to the collar, so there is no hard edge beside the thumb
Collar one 25.4 mm round plate the strips' top stands in, wide enough to cover the band's corners, then two 22.2 mm round plates with the reference's 4.87 mm bore; the cap rests on them, flush

Every hole is where the reference's is, measured from the reed's seat, which is what the notes depend on. The sizes differ because a hole here goes through a 3 mm strip, where the reference's walls are 5 to 7 mm. A hole through a thinner wall sounds sharper, so it has to be smaller for the same note. A 4.5 mm bore, the smallest that takes the reference's 4.15 mm hole, would need holes of 2.5 to 3.2 mm. The 5.5 mm bore is the size at which they come back to the reference's, 3.2 to 4.1 mm. Each is solved so its note sounds where the reference's does, in the model, and the bore is 3.8 mm longer at the sole to bring low G there too.

The model is used only for the difference, not the pitch. It cannot reproduce the reference's own scale: at the reed allowance that fits best, 130 mm, it is still about 40 cents out across the scale, low A at about 255Hz. So it is asked only how far the square bore and the thin wall move each note from where the reference's is, and it plays at whatever pitch the reference plays. The reference's maker had not yet printed it, so that pitch is the real chanter's they measured, not one anyone has checked on the print.

That difference depends on how the reed is modelled. The holes hardly move with the reed allowance's length: at 100 mm or 160 mm instead of 130 mm, low A's hole changes by 0.1 mm and the others by less. They do move with where it is put. Here the 130 mm is taken as more of the square bore, as long as the reference's allowance. Taken instead as the reference's own 4.87 mm round bore above the square, low G would want the bore 288 mm long, not 242 mm. Neither is measured, so the first one built and played settles it; expect to tune it.

Shellac barely moves the pitch. Dipping the chanter four times in 3 to 5 lb cut leaves perhaps 50 to 100µm of shellac on each surface. That is an estimate, not a measurement: the first coat soaks into the ply, and the film depends on the cut and how it drains. It narrows the bore from 5.5 mm to between 5.4 and 5.3 mm, and every hole by the same 0.1 to 0.2 mm. In the model that flattens the notes by 1 to 3 cents, the high notes most. Watch for shellac pooling in the bore's corners and bridging the finger holes: blow through the chanter after each dip, and clear each hole with a toothpick. The seat narrows the same way, to about 5.4 mm, still over the largest staple.

High A has room. The thumb reaches up past its hole, so the band leaves the back bare. The side pieces of the band are cut away along a quarter circle where they meet the back, so they slope up to the collar rather than ending square beside the thumb. Nothing stands proud of the back strip until the collar, 22.1 mm above the hole's centre. On the reference chanter, its collar is 20.6 mm above.

Cut the sheet, and glue the strips as in the strip chanter's first two steps, round a 5.5 mm rod or waxed drill bit. Then:

  1. Press the jig's base and two rings together on two 2 mm pins, and line the cup with packing tape or wax so the glue cannot stick to it. Stand the chanter in it on its sole. Glue the foot's layers in round the strips one at a time, the circle centring each. Each piece runs round one corner of the circle to the inner corner beyond. Each layer turns the other way from the one below, so no seam runs through.
  2. Glue the band round the strips' top 10 mm: the wide piece across the front, the two narrow ones on the sides, flush with the back, their curved corners at the back and down, away from the collar.
  3. Press the collar's two round plates and the tenon together on two 2 mm pins, and cut the pins flush with the collar's bottom face.
  4. Glue the strips' top 3 mm into the collar's socket plate, and the collar's round plates onto it. The pins' ends land on the strips' top corners, inside the socket plate.
  5. Glue the cap's 40 rings and its top plate together, top plate last. Lay them in the inside corner of two straight boards while the glue sets, which lines up their outsides. Then glue the mouthpiece on as for the other chanters.
  6. Seal the chanter by dipping it four times in 3 to 5 lb cut shellac, as above. After each dip blow through the bore and clear each hole with a toothpick. Brush the inside of the cap.
  7. Push the reed's staple into the seat. Wrap the tenon with hemp or tape until the cap slides on snugly and rests on the collar.

The standard octagon

The standard strip chanter's holes on eight strips instead of four: the same measured chanter, its holes where that chanter's are, on an octagonal bore. An octagon's faces are narrow, so the holes are slots along the strips.

Why slots. Each strip is 5.8 mm wide outside but only 3.3 mm inside, and a hole needs 1.5 mm of ply each side, so nothing wider than 2.8 mm fits. The notes want holes of 4.7 to 6 mm, so each is a slot of that hole's area, as long as it needs, up to 12 mm, which a finger pad still covers. The model treats a slot as the round hole of its area, as it does the stacked octagon's slots. A bigger octagon does not help: its holes grow faster than its faces. A 12 mm octagon would need slots about 4.5 mm across and up to 17 mm long. Nor can it be smaller, nearer the reference's bore: below 8 mm the strip opposite each slot, which owns neither corner there, is under 1.5 mm wide (1.35 mm at 7.5 mm, 0.31 mm at 5 mm), and the collar's pins no longer land on the strips' top.

The joints. The strips meet at 135°. Where a strip owns a corner it runs out to it; where its neighbour does, it stops 2.12 mm short, square to its face. Each strip owns one of its two corners at a time, in turn, so most of its length is 3.7 mm wide outside. Round each slot its own strip owns both corners, 1.5 mm past the slot. Eight strips round eight corners can't all keep one each there, so the strip opposite owns neither, and is 1.56 mm wide for the slot's length: the back strip opposite each finger hole, and the front opposite the thumb's, 97 mm in all. Once glued, those stretches are held on both edges. Every corner also leaves a small groove along the bore, a 0.88 mm right triangle, and the bore's area counts all eight.

The model's question is bigger here. The bore is three times the reference's area, where the square's is 1.6 times, so the model is asked for a bigger difference. It also depends on how the reed is modelled. Like the square, the octagon takes the reed's 130 mm allowance as more of its own bore. Taken instead as the reference's round bore above it, low G would want the octagon 328 mm long, not 242 mm, and the square 288 mm. Neither is measured, so treat this chanter as the more experimental of the two and expect to tune it.

Building it:

  1. Cut the sheet. Dry-fit the eight strips round a waxed 7.5 mm dowel or drill bit. Each strip's two edges have different finger patterns, so it goes in only one way. The front strip carries the seven finger slots and the back strip the thumb's.
  2. Glue the strips round the dowel. Hold them with tape or rubber bands while the glue sets, and wipe off any that squeezes into the bore.
  3. Press the collar's two round plates and the tenon together on two 2 mm pins, and cut the pins flush with the collar's bottom face.
  4. Glue the strips' top 3 mm into the collar's socket plate, and the collar's round plates onto it. The pins' ends land on the strips' top.
  5. Slide the two foot plates on from the sole and glue them flush with it, on a flat board.
  6. Glue the cap's 40 rings and its top plate together, top plate last, in the inside corner of two straight boards. Then glue the mouthpiece on as for the other chanters.
  7. Seal the chanter by dipping it four times in 3 to 5 lb cut shellac, as for the standard strip chanter. After each dip blow through the bore, so the shellac fills the corner grooves without pooling, and clear each slot. Brush the inside of the cap.
  8. Push the reed's staple into the seat. Wrap the tenon with hemp or tape until the cap slides on snugly and rests on the collar.

How it is built

The stacked octagon, from the top down:

  • The mouthpiece, 24 round plates with a 4.5 mm blowing hole, shaped as the measured practice chanter's top piece is: a 45° shoulder off the cap, a step to 16.5 mm, a step to 14.3 mm, then a long taper from 12.4 to 11.0 mm and a rounded lip. It is 72 mm long, the reference's 71.5 mm. Each plate is cut to the reference's diameter at its own mid-height, so the long taper goes down in steps of under 0.1 mm. The plates are too narrow for pins, so they are glued, lined up on a rod through the blowing hole.
  • The cap: two top plates, twelve plates round a 13 mm chamber for the reed's blades, and five plates whose hole is the body's own octagon, 0.3 mm larger, to slide over the top of the body. It is the body's shape grown 6.3 mm outward, pressed together on two pins in its corners.
  • The seat: nine plates with the reference's 5.57 mm round seat for the reed's staple. Practice-chanter staples run 4 to 4.8 mm across, and the 4.74 mm bore below stops only the largest, so the staple is held by its hemp, as on the reference.
  • The bore: one plate for every 3 mm, the octagon through each, down to the open sole. Eight of them carry the finger holes.

Every body plate has the same two rod holes, placed so that turning or flipping the plate puts them somewhere else: a plate threads on only one way, so no slot can end up facing the wrong way. The rods are clamped by a nut and washer at each end. A pin pressed through 88 plates would never go in, and nothing is glued. The cap is not on the rods, since it comes off to set the reed. It slides over the top five plates of the body and rests on the two top nuts.

Before you cut

Measure the ply. The stack multiplies the stock's error by every plate in it. 2.9 mm ply makes the stacked octagon's bore 8 mm short, and every note 12 to 44 cents sharp in the model, low G most. Measure a few places on the sheet with calipers, then pass the average as --plate=. The generator re-solves every hole on that grid, and the plate count follows.

The reed's length is a guess. The model treats the reed and staple as extra closed bore: 130 mm on the stacked octagon and the two standard chanters, the figure that best fits the measured chanter, and 20 mm on the strip chanter. Neither is a measured figure. A reed pushed deeper into its seat plays sharper, and one pulled out plays flatter. That is how every practice chanter is pitched, and it shifts the whole scale together, but it cannot fix one note against another. strip_chanter --reed= sets the strip chanter's figure if you measure your own.

The model does not match a real chanter. It is a transfer-matrix model with textbook tone-hole and end corrections. Given the measured chanter that the standard strip chanter copies, it cannot reproduce that chanter's scale at any reed allowance. The closest, at 130 mm, is still about 40 cents out, and at the 20 mm the strip chanter was designed with, the reference's scale comes out stretched by hundreds of cents. So the strip chanter may play well out of tune, with the upper notes sharpest, until a real chanter has been played and the model fitted to it. The stacked octagon and the two standard chanters avoid most of this by copying a real chanter and using the model only for the difference. For the strip chanter, expect to do the Tuning in earnest.

The slots are cut exactly to width, so a kerf other than the default 0.13 mm changes every hole. Pass yours with --kerf=.

Building it

You need two lengths of M2 threaded rod, four M2 nuts and washers, a few centimetres of 2 mm brass rod for the pins, high-cut shellac, and a plastic practice-chanter reed.

  1. Cut the sheet. The generator prints the stacking order: 9 seat, 10 bore, hA, 3 bore, G, 6 bore, F, 7 bore, E, 7 bore, D, 8 bore, C, 7 bore, B, 10 bore, A, 13 bore. Here "seat" and "bore" are runs of the plain plates, and the letters are the hole plates.
  2. Cut the rods to the length the generator prints: 271 mm. Put a nut and washer on one end of each.
  3. Thread the plates on from the sole up, in the reverse of that order. Brush thin shellac on each face as it goes on. The slots all face the same way because the rods only let them.
  4. Put the top washers and nuts on and tighten them evenly, just enough to close every seam. Cut the rods off flush with the top nuts, or the cap will rest on the rods instead of the nuts. Let the shellac dry.
  5. If the generator printed a hole to drill (at 3 mm ply there is none), drill it at its V mark, starting a size under the printed figure. Then seal the bore: draw a pipe cleaner wet with shellac through it a few times, and let it dry. Brush the inside of the cap the same way.
  6. Press the cap's plates together on two pins through its corners. Stack the mouthpiece's plates, widest first, on a 4.5 mm drill shank pushed down through the cap's top, gluing each one, and pull the shank out before the glue sets. Sand the steps of the long taper smooth if you like.
  7. Push the reed's staple into the seat. Wrap it with hemp if it is loose. Put the cap on.

Tuning

Tune low A first, by moving the reed in or out of its seat. Then go up the scale:

  • A note that is flat wants its hole bigger. File the slot wider a little at a time, or open the drill up a size.
  • A note that is sharp wants its hole smaller. Line part of the slot with tape, or with a sliver of veneer and shellac.
  • Low G has no hole. Tape across part of the open sole flattens it.

Widening a hole moves its own note most, about 10 cents for a fifth wider in the model. The notes either side of it move a cent or two, so go up the scale once, then check every note again and touch up.

  • every note is the reference's in the model, and low G is as near as whole plates allow;

  • every hole is where the reference's is, to the plate;

  • every finger hole is in its own plate, at its centre;
  • every hole is cuttable or drillable;
  • every slot runs the width it is tuned for;
  • every rod and pin keeps ply between it and every cut;

  • every sheet fits the bed;
  • no two parts on a sheet come closer than the gap;
  • every part is on a sheet.

The crate borrows the kerf, the Part type and the packing from the square flute's square_flute.rs, the mitred offset from the four-hole ocarinas' four_hole_ocarinas.rs, the path writer from swept-curve's ribbon_bore.rs, each note's search from bore-tmm's src/models.rs and the 3D page from lasermade-tools. So square-flute, four-hole-ocarinas, swept-curve, bore-tmm and lasermade-tools must be checked out beside this repository.
